\begin{abstract}
Hash tables are foundational data structures in transaction indexing systems with high turnover rates, where items are continuously inserted and deleted. While open addressing with Linear Probing is cache-friendly, the accumulation of tombstones degrades lookup latency, especially for unsuccessful queries, necessitating costly periodic table rebuilds. In this study, we empirically evaluate four collision resolution strategies across millions of operations in Java: Separate Chaining, Linear Probing with tombstones, Linear Probing with backward-shift deletion, and Robin Hood hashing (both with and without early-stopping). We address three research questions regarding load factor scaling (RQ1), tombstone pollution dynamics (RQ2), and mechanism decomposition in Robin Hood hashing (RQ3). Our empirical findings demonstrate that: (1) for RQ1, while Separate Chaining keeps probe counts below 2 at every tested load (up to $\alpha = 0.95$), Linear Probing is faster only for successful lookups at $\alpha = 0.50$ ($36.9$ vs.\ $54.0$\,ns/op) and uses $1.6\times$--$2.8\times$ less measured heap per key, with its advantage disappearing at higher loads, larger tables and clustered keys; (2) in the single-seed RQ2 campaign, tombstone accumulation depletes empty slots down to $0.04\%$, raising miss probes to $2,848.99$ ($1,141.5\times$ the Clean Table value at $C=4M$) and miss lookup time to $18.22$ seconds per one-million-query batch; and (3) for RQ3, Robin Hood hashing with early exit slashes unsuccessful search depth by up to $73.8\%$ under heavy load ($\alpha = 0.80$), while backward-shift deletion (Knuth Algorithm R and Robin Hood) removes tombstones and rebuild pauses entirely.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Hash tables are widely used to build in-memory indexes, including indexes for
records that are continuously inserted, deleted, and queried. In such
high-churn workloads, the collision-resolution strategy affects not only
lookup cost but also the cost of keeping the table searchable after deletions.
These effects are especially important for unsuccessful lookups and for
open-addressed tables whose probe sequences can be affected by deleted slots.

Separate Chaining (SC) stores colliding keys in per-bucket lists, whereas
Linear Probing (LP) places keys in adjacent array slots. LP offers contiguous
accesses, but deletion must preserve the probe paths of remaining keys.
Tombstones provide one solution, although their accumulation can lengthen
searches and motivate periodic rebuilding. Backward-shift deletion instead
repairs a cluster in place. Robin Hood hashing further redistributes keys
according to their probe distances and can use those distances to terminate
unsuccessful searches early. These designs therefore make different trade-offs
between lookup work and deletion maintenance.

This paper evaluates these trade-offs with a common Java hash-table interface
and controlled, reproducible workloads. We organize the study around three
research questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1:} How do SC and LP with tombstones compare as the load
    factor and table capacity vary, including under a clustered-key workload?
    \item \textbf{RQ2:} How does tombstone accumulation during repeated
    delete--insert operations affect lookup behavior, and how does it compare
    with a clean table containing the same live keys?
    \item \textbf{RQ3:} How does Robin Hood hashing compare with LP using
    backward-shift deletion under churn, and what is the contribution of
    early-exit lookup to Robin Hood search cost?
\end{itemize}

The study makes three contributions. First, it implements SC, LP with
tombstones and rebuilding, LP with backward-shift deletion, and Robin Hood
hashing in a shared interface. Second, it defines repeatable workloads and
separates elapsed-time measurements from probe-count measurements. Third, it
uses a Robin Hood variant without early exit to isolate that lookup mechanism
while retaining Robin Hood insertion and deletion. The paper reports measured
results separately from this methodological description; no performance
outcome is assumed in advance.

\section{Background and Related Work}
Peterson's work on random-access storage is an early reference for open
addressing and linear probing \cite{peterson}. With LP, a collision is resolved
by examining successive slots, so keys with the same or nearby home buckets
can form contiguous clusters. Knuth analyzes this primary-clustering effect
and gives classical expected probe counts for a table with load factor
$\alpha$ under the standard no-deletion model:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}[P_{\mathrm{hit}}] \approx
    \frac{1}{2}\left(1 + \frac{1}{1-\alpha}\right), \qquad
    \mathbb{E}[P_{\mathrm{miss}}] \approx
    \frac{1}{2}\left(1 + \frac{1}{(1-\alpha)^2}\right).
\end{equation}
These expressions provide a theoretical reference for clean LP tables; they
do not directly model the additional state introduced by tombstones or
repeated updates \cite{knuth}.

Deletion requires care in open-addressed tables: changing a deleted slot
directly to empty can make a later key unreachable along its probe sequence.
Tombstones preserve that sequence, but leave non-empty markers that searches
must pass and that a rebuild can remove. Knuth's Algorithm R instead scans
the affected cluster and moves a key into the current hole only when its
probe path permits the move \cite{knuth}. Sedgewick and Wayne describe a
related approach that removes and reinserts the keys following the deleted
slot \cite{sedgewick}. Both approaches maintain lookup correctness without
retaining tombstones, but perform different work during deletion. This study
uses Algorithm R for its LP backshift implementation.

Celis, Larson, and Munro introduced Robin Hood hashing, which compares the
distance from initial bucket (DIB) of an incoming key with that of the key
already occupying a slot \cite{celis}. If the incoming key has traveled
farther, the keys exchange positions and insertion continues with the
displaced key. The resulting DIB information also supports an unsuccessful
lookup stopping rule: if the query's probe distance exceeds the resident
key's DIB, the query cannot occur later in the probe sequence. Robin Hood
deletion can shift following keys backward while their DIB is positive,
avoiding tombstones.

Prior work establishes these algorithms and their analytical properties.
Our contribution is an empirical comparison of implementations under a
shared Java interface and repeatable workloads. In particular, the
\texttt{rh-noearly} variant retains Robin Hood insertion and backward-shift
deletion but disables the DIB-based lookup stopping rule, enabling a
mechanism-level comparison within the same table design.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Collision Resolution Implementations}
All hash tables implement a uniform \texttt{HashTable} interface operating on 64-bit keys using Fibonacci hashing $h(k) = (k \cdot \phi) \ggg (64 - p)$ for table capacity $M = 2^p$, where $\phi = \texttt{0x9E3779B97F4A7C15}$ is the 64-bit golden-ratio constant (a signed Java \texttt{long} with wrap-around multiplication). We evaluate four families of designs (five configurations in total):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Separate Chaining (\texttt{sc})}: Singly-linked list per bucket.
    \item \textbf{Linear Probing Tombstone (\texttt{lp-tomb})}: Parallel primitive arrays (\texttt{long[] keys}, \texttt{int[] values}, \texttt{byte[] state}), tracking tombstones with periodic rebuild threshold $\tau \cdot M$.
    \item \textbf{Linear Probing Backshift (\texttt{lp-backshift})}: In-place cluster shift using Knuth's Algorithm R upon deletion.
    \item \textbf{Robin Hood Hashing (\texttt{rh} \& \texttt{rh-noearly})}: Full Robin Hood table tracking DIB per slot with displacement swaps and backward-shift deletion. The variant \texttt{rh-noearly} ablates the early-exit mechanism to isolate its performance contribution.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Justification for Knuth Algorithm R in \texttt{lp-backshift}}
Between Knuth Algorithm R \cite{knuth} and Sedgewick's cluster re-insertion \cite{sedgewick}, our study adopts \textbf{Knuth Algorithm R} for three methodological reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Bounded scan}: Algorithm R makes one forward scan of the affected cluster, performing $\mathcal{O}(K)$ slot checks and at most a constant number of moves per visited key for a cluster of length $K$. Cluster re-insertion instead invokes a probe sequence for each key moved out of the cluster, so it can repeat slot inspections.
    \item \textit{Direct movement}: Algorithm R moves a key directly into the current hole only when its home bucket permits that move. This avoids the explicit remove-and-reinsert steps of the alternative; cache or wall-clock advantages are not assumed without measurement.
    \item \textit{Circular correctness}: The test of whether a key's home bucket $r$ lies in the circular interval $(i,j]$ determines whether moving it to the hole $i$ preserves its linear-probing search path, including when the cluster wraps around the array boundary.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Robin Hood and Backshift Implementations}
The Robin Hood variants use fixed-capacity arrays for keys, values, slot
states, and DIB values. Insertion begins at the key's Fibonacci-hash bucket
with DIB zero. At each occupied slot, the incoming key takes the slot when its
DIB is greater than the resident key's DIB; insertion then continues with the
displaced key. Probe indices wrap around the power-of-two table capacity.

For lookup, \texttt{rh} checks for a matching key and an empty slot, and also
terminates an unsuccessful search when the current probe distance exceeds the
DIB stored in the slot. The \texttt{rh-noearly} ablation uses the same
insertion and deletion procedures but omits this DIB-based stopping condition;
it continues until it finds the key, reaches an empty slot, or has examined
the table. Thus, the comparison isolates early exit from the other Robin Hood
mechanisms. Both variants remove a key by shifting subsequent entries with
positive DIB backward one slot, decrementing each shifted DIB, and stopping
at an empty slot or an entry with DIB zero.

The \texttt{lp-backshift} implementation uses ordinary linear probing and
Knuth's Algorithm R for deletion. Starting from the deleted slot (the current
hole), it scans the following cluster. For a key at slot $j$ with home bucket
$r$, it moves the key into the hole at $i$ only if $r$ is not in the circular
interval $(i,j]$; the moved key's former slot becomes the new hole. The scan
ends at an empty slot or when it returns to the starting slot after a full
cycle, and the final hole is marked empty. This interval test handles clusters
that wrap around the end of the array and no tombstone state is introduced.

\subsection{RQ2 Workload and Clean Table Reference Methodology}
To rigorously quantify the performance penalty of tombstone accumulation in Linear Probing without confounding variables, RQ2 implements a controlled churn workload on a fixed-capacity table $M = 2^{20} = 1,048,576$ with active load factor $\alpha = 0.50$ ($N = 524,288$). The workload lifecycle proceeds as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Initial Population}: The table is initially populated with $N$ unique 64-bit keys generated pseudo-randomly using \texttt{SplittableRandom(seed)}. An active key pool is maintained in a contiguous array \texttt{aliveKeys} alongside a tracking set \texttt{seenKeys} to guarantee global uniqueness.
    \item \textbf{Constant-Load Churn Cycles}: The workload executes $C$ continuous churn cycles. In each cycle, an existing key is chosen uniformly at random from \texttt{aliveKeys} in $\mathcal{O}(1)$ time via the swap-with-last removal technique, and deleted from the table using \texttt{remove(k)}. Concurrently, a newly generated unique key is inserted via \texttt{put(k, v)}. This guarantees that active load remains strictly invariant at $N = 524,288$ while exercising repeated slot recycling.
    \item \textbf{Milestone Snapshots}: Telemetry is recorded across six predefined churn milestones: $C \in \{0, 0.25M, 0.5M, M, 2M, 4M\}$. At each milestone, the runner records table state metrics (live count, tombstone count, empty count, and probe depth).
    \item \textbf{Clean Table Baseline}: At every milestone $C$, a companion pristine \textit{Clean Table} is reconstructed from scratch by inserting the exact current set of live keys in sequential array index order into a fresh table of identical capacity $M = 2^{20}$. Comparing the contaminated table against the Clean Table under identical query sequences completely isolates the degradation attributable solely to tombstones from key distribution effects.
    \item \textbf{Isolated Query Passes}: At each milestone, separate passes of $Q = 1,000,000$ hit and $Q = 1,000,000$ miss lookups are executed in dedicated JVM processes to benchmark both probe depth and wall-clock latency. Since $Q>N$, hit keys are sampled uniformly with replacement from the active key set; miss keys are generated to be absent from every key seen in the workload.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{RQ3 Workload and Measurement}
For RQ3, each table is first populated with
$N=\operatorname{round}(\alpha M)$ unique keys generated from the specified
seed. The workload then performs $C$ churn cycles. Each cycle selects one
currently live key for deletion and inserts a new key not used earlier in the
workload. This keeps the live-key count approximately constant while exposing
the deletion policy to repeated updates. The same capacity, load factor,
seed, and cycle count are used when comparing \texttt{lp-tomb},
\texttt{lp-backshift}, \texttt{rh}, and \texttt{rh-noearly}. The tombstone
table uses a rebuild threshold of $\tau=0.2$ at $\alpha=0.50$ and $\tau=0.1$ at $\alpha=0.80$; the other implementations do not
use tombstones or rebuilding.

After the churn phase, the benchmark runs separate hit and miss query passes.
The hit pass queries all live keys; the miss pass uses generated keys absent
from the live set. Table construction and churn happen before lookup timing.
Timing and probe counting are collected in separate JVM runs: time mode
measures elapsed lookup time, while count mode reports average probes per
query. The benchmark harness records all empirical telemetry into a standardized
21-column CSV schema capturing execution status, table construction time (\texttt{build\_ms}),
query latency (\texttt{elapsed\_ms}), memory footprints, deterministic probe counts
(\texttt{ops\_count}), input cryptographic hashes, and verification checksums for reproducibility.

\section{Experimental Setup}
All experimental benchmarks were conducted on an Intel Core i5-11500H processor (6 cores, 12 threads @ 2.90 GHz base, 4.60 GHz turbo) with 32 GB DDR4 RAM running 64-bit Windows 11 Pro. The software runtime environment is the 64-bit OpenJDK HotSpot JVM (Eclipse Temurin; exact build recorded in the repository README) launched with strict JVM flags: \texttt{-Xmx2g -XX:+UseG1GC}.

For RQ2, the evaluation is executed across the six churn milestones $C \in \{0, 0.25M, 0.5M, M, 2M, 4M\}$ at $M = 2^{20} = 1,048,576$ and $\alpha = 0.50$ ($N = 524,288$) with seed $42$ and one repeat. Each configuration executes separate hit and miss query passes of $1,000,000$ operations for both the contaminated table (\texttt{base}) and the reference baseline (\texttt{clean\_table}) across both measurement modes (\texttt{count} and \texttt{time}), yielding 48 JVM trials constrained by an automated 60-second process timeout.

For RQ3, measurements evaluate moderate load $\alpha=0.50$ ($\tau=0.20$) and high-load stress $\alpha=0.80$ ($\tau=0.10$) across checkpoints $C \in \{0, M/4, M/2, M, 2M, 4M\}$ using seeds $42, 1337, 20261019$, comparing \texttt{lp-tomb}, \texttt{lp-backshift}, \texttt{rh}, and \texttt{rh-noearly}. Each process uses a 2 GB maximum heap and a 60-second safety timeout, recording build and lookup phases independently.

\subsection{RQ1: Separate Chaining vs. Linear Probing under Increasing Load and Scale}
\label{sec:results-rq1}

To evaluate how Separate Chaining (\texttt{sc}) and Linear Probing (\texttt{lp-tomb} without deletions) behave as load and table capacity grow, we conducted two systematic experiments and an adversarial clustering benchmark under controlled JVM settings.

\subsubsection{Experiment A: Sensitivity to Load Factor ($\alpha$)}

We then executed $5\times 10^6$ successful lookups (hit) and $5\times 10^6$ unsuccessful lookups (miss) using disjoint key sets verified by a pre-filter \texttt{HashSet}.

\subsubsection{Experiment B: Scalability across Table Capacities ($M$)}
At fixed load factor $\alpha = 0.75$, table capacity was scaled from $M = 2^{18}$ to $M = 2^{23}$ ($2.62\times 10^5$ to $8.38\times 10^6$ slots). Average probe counts remained invariant across all table dimensions ($2.495$--$2.507$ for LP hit, $1.375$--$1.377$ for SC hit), consistent with probe complexity being scale-invariant in $M$. Per-operation hit latency, however, grew with $M$ for both tables: from $M = 2^{18}$ to $2^{23}$, \texttt{sc} rose from $36.9$ to $85.7$\,ns/op ($+132\%$) and \texttt{lp-tomb} from $35.5$ to $131.9$\,ns/op ($+272\%$). Between $M = 2^{20}$ and $2^{21}$ the increases were similar ($+35.8\%$ for \texttt{sc}, $+37.8\%$ for \texttt{lp-tomb}), and from $M = 2^{20}$ onward \texttt{lp-tomb} was slower than \texttt{sc} (for example $104.0$ vs.\ $66.9$\,ns/op at $M = 2^{22}$). The growth is compatible with the working set outgrowing the CPU caches (L3 is $12$\,MB on our reference machine), but no hardware counters were collected and each configuration has a single repeat, so we do not attribute a cause.

\subsubsection{Impact of Clustered Key Distribution}
Under an adversarial benchmark where $10\%$ of keys were concentrated into $M/64$ slots ($h(k) < M/64$) and the remaining $90\%$ remained uniformly distributed, \texttt{lp-tomb} suffered a catastrophic increase in probe depth at $\alpha = 0.75$: hit probes rose from $2.50$ to $2{,}806$ at $M = 2^{18}$ ($\approx 1{,}124\times$) and from $2.51$ to $11{,}265$ at $M = 2^{20}$ ($\approx 4{,}493\times$), while miss probes rose from $8.53$ to $27{,}803$ at $M = 2^{20}$. Because the probe depth grows in proportion to $M$, the dense region forms one cluster whose length scales with the table. For Separate Chaining, hit probes rose by only $16.9\%$ ($1.377 \to 1.610$) at $M = 2^{18}$ and $17.3\%$ ($1.375 \to 1.612$) at $M = 2^{20}$, and miss probes were unchanged ($\approx 0.75$), because collisions stay inside the over-allocated buckets and do not spill into neighboring buckets.

\subsubsection{Discussion: The Java Memory Gap and Probe Count vs. Latency Paradox}
The experimental results show that probe count and wall-clock latency can rank the two tables differently. Separate Chaining requires fewer probes at all tested loads, yet Linear Probing is faster in wall-clock time only for successful lookups at $\alpha = 0.50$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:rq1_latency}): $36.9$\,ns/op versus $54.0$\,ns/op for \texttt{sc} ($-31.5\%$), despite inspecting more slots ($1.50$ vs.\ $1.25$ probes). At $\alpha = 0.75$ Separate Chaining is already faster on hits ($39.9$ vs.\ $46.2$\,ns/op), at $\alpha = 0.95$ it is much faster ($50.7$ vs.\ $84.0$\,ns/op), and for unsuccessful lookups it is faster at every load (for example $24.7$ vs.\ $41.4$\,ns/op at $\alpha = 0.50$ and $47.7$ vs.\ $301.4$\,ns/op at $\alpha = 0.95$). Three architectural mechanisms plausibly explain the $\alpha = 0.50$ hit-latency advantage and the memory gap, although our measurements do not isolate them:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Object Header Overhead and Pointer Chasing in \texttt{sc}:} In a 64-bit HotSpot JVM with Compressed OOPs, each \texttt{Node} object consists of a 12-byte header, an 8-byte \texttt{long} key, a 4-byte \texttt{int} value, and a 4-byte compressed reference (\texttt{Node next}); these 28 bytes are padded by the JVM to 32 bytes of heap memory per entry. Because nodes are allocated dynamically via \texttt{new Node()}, they are scattered across the JVM nursery and tenured heap. Traversing \texttt{next} pointers requires dereferencing non-contiguous heap addresses, causing recurring CPU L1/L2 cache misses and memory bus stalls.
    \item \textbf{Spatial Locality and Hardware Prefetching in \texttt{lp}:} Linear Probing stores keys, values, and states in flat primitive arrays (\texttt{long[]}, \texttt{int[]}, \texttt{byte[]}). Probing consecutive array indices loads contiguous 64-byte CPU cache lines, enabling hardware stream prefetchers to load subsequent slots ahead of use and reduce memory stalls. Consequently, inspecting an adjacent array slot in \texttt{lp-tomb} is expected to be considerably cheaper than dereferencing a linked node in \texttt{sc}; we measured per-lookup time, not per-probe cost.
    \item \textbf{Garbage Collection (GC) and Memory Footprint:} Populating \texttt{sc} with millions of individual \texttt{Node} objects creates severe allocation churn, triggering frequent minor GC cycles during table construction. By the object layout above, \texttt{sc} consumes $32$\,bytes per node plus $4$\,bytes per bucket pointer, versus $13$\,bytes per slot for \texttt{lp-tomb}. The analytical per-slot ratio is $(4+32\alpha)/13$, i.e.\ about $1.5\times$ at $\alpha=0.50$ and $2.7\times$ at $\alpha=0.95$. The measured values in Fig.~\ref{fig:rq1_memory} come from process-level heap telemetry (Section~\ref{sec:threats}), so they are indicative rather than exact. They give $31.8$--$38.6$\,B/key for \texttt{sc} versus $11.8$--$24.6$\,B/key for \texttt{lp-tomb}, i.e.\ $1.6\times$ at $\alpha=0.50$ and $2.7\times$--$2.8\times$ for $\alpha \ge 0.75$.
\end{enumerate}

In summary, probe count alone is an incomplete metric for performance evaluation in managed language runtimes. In our data, Linear Probing's latency advantage is confined to successful lookups at $\alpha = 0.50$, while its memory-density advantage persists at all tested loads. Separate Chaining is the safer choice when $\alpha \ge 0.75$, when unsuccessful lookups dominate, when tables are large, or when hash functions are susceptible to pathological clustering.

\subsection{RQ2: Tombstone Pollution Dynamics and Periodic Rebuilding}

To quantify this degradation under continuous turnover, we evaluate \texttt{lp-tomb} ($\tau = \infty$, no rebuild) across six churn milestones $C \in \{0, 0.25M, 0.5M, M, 2M, 4M\}$ with $M = 2^{20} = 1,048,576$ and active load factor $\alpha = 0.50$ ($N = 524,288$). At each checkpoint, separate passes of $Q = 1,000,000$ hit and $Q = 1,000,000$ miss lookups benchmark the contaminated table against an identical pristine \textit{Clean Table} containing the exact same active keys without tombstones. Hit keys are sampled with replacement because $Q > N$. The empirical data across all six checkpoints reveal four fundamental engineering and algorithmic insights:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Slot Contamination Dynamics and Empty Slot Depletion}: As churn progresses, deletions repeatedly convert live slots into tombstones. Although subsequent insertions can reclaim tombstone slots, the uniform distribution of key hashes causes tombstones to disperse throughout the entire table. As seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:rq2_tombstone_dynamics}(a), the tombstone proportion escalates rapidly from $0.00\%$ to $18.79\%$ at $C = 0.25M$, $41.91\%$ at $C = 1M$, and saturates at $49.96\%$ ($523,876$ slots) at $C = 4M$. Concurrently, the proportion of pristine \texttt{EMPTY} slots undergoes exponential extinction, plummeting from $50.00\%$ ($524,288$ slots) down to $8.09\%$ at $C = 1M$, $1.36\%$ at $C = 2M$, and reaching a critical nadir of only \textbf{0.04\%} ($412$ remaining slots out of $1,048,576$) at $C = 4M$. At this stage, $99.92\%$ of the capacity available outside active keys is contaminated by tombstones.

    \item \textbf{Asymmetric Probe Divergence (Hit Plateau vs. Catastrophic Miss Explosion)}: The impact of tombstone saturation on search depth is severely asymmetric between query types:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Successful Lookups (Hit Probes)}: Hit queries remain well-behaved, increasing from $1.500$ to $2.346$ probes (a $1.56\times$ increase). Because a successful lookup terminates immediately upon matching the target key, it only traverses tombstones that displaced the key from its initial hash bucket within its localized cluster.
        \item \textit{Unsuccessful Lookups (Miss Probes)}: In standard Linear Probing, an unsuccessful lookup must probe sequentially until encountering an \texttt{EMPTY} slot. When empty slots are depleted to $0.04\%$, miss searches cannot terminate early and are forced to traverse massive contiguous clusters of tombstones. As detailed in Table~\ref{tab:rq2_results} and Fig.~\ref{fig:rq2_tombstone_dynamics}(b), miss probe depth explodes non-linearly: from $2.503$ probes at $C=0$ to $17.237$ at $C=1M$, $86.070$ at $C=2M$, and reaching \textbf{2,848.989 probes} at $C=4M$. This is approximately a $1,138\times$ increase over the initial contaminated table and a \textbf{1,141.51$\times$} increase over the Clean Table at $C=4M$.
        \item \textit{Clean Table Immunity}: In contrast, the reference \textit{Clean Table} containing the identical $524,288$ live keys maintains probe efficiency across all milestones: $1.496 - 1.502$ hit probes and $2.490 - 2.503$ miss probes, close to Knuth's classical theoretical bounds $\mathbb{E}[P_{\mathrm{hit}}] \approx \frac{1}{2}(1 + \frac{1}{1-\alpha}) = 1.500$ and $\mathbb{E}[P_{\mathrm{miss}}] \approx \frac{1}{2}(1 + \frac{1}{(1-\alpha)^2}) = 2.500$.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Lookup Latency Kinetics}: The one-million-query miss batch grows from $48.05$ ms at $C=0$ to \textbf{18,223.54 ms} ($18.22$ seconds) at $C=4M$. At the final milestone this is $203.87\times$ the Clean Table time of $89.39$ ms. Hit-time measurements are noisy (for example, the Clean Table takes $131.00$ ms at $C=4M$ versus $34.93$ ms at $C=2M$), so the probe-count trend provides the more stable mechanism-level evidence in this single-repeat campaign.

    \item \textbf{Timeout Analysis, Asymptotic Transition to $O(M)$, and Periodic Rebuilding ($\tau$)}:
    The automated runner applies a $60$-second timeout to each complete JVM process, including startup, table construction, churn, and queries. The longest process in the corrected one-million-query campaign took $46.044$ seconds (the \texttt{base}, $C=4M$, miss-time run), leaving $13.956$ seconds before the limit. Consequently, all 48 measured runs achieved the \texttt{ok} status with zero timeout exceptions. The CSV's \texttt{elapsed\_ms} records lookup time only; it is not the full process duration.
    
    However, the trajectory reveals a dangerous asymptotic property: when $C \to \infty$ without rebuilding, the number of empty slots approaches zero ($\text{EMPTY} \to 0$). In that limit, every unsuccessful query is forced to scan all $M = 2^{20}$ slots in a full circular pass before failing, degrading per-query complexity from expected $\mathcal{O}(1)$ to deterministic $\mathcal{O}(M)$. In real-time or multi-threaded production workloads, the measured $203.87\times$ miss-latency surge at $C=4M$ illustrates the resulting timeout risk.
    
    These results motivate \textbf{periodic rebuilding}: reconstructing the table whenever the tombstone count reaches $\tau \cdot M$ resets all tombstones to zero. RQ2 itself does not evaluate rebuilding; its effect is measured in RQ3, where $\tau=0.20$ ($\alpha=0.50$) and $\tau=0.10$ ($\alpha=0.80$) trigger 14 and 23 rebuilds over $4M$ churn cycles (about one rebuild per $3\times10^5$ and $1.8\times10^5$ cycles). Rebuilding bounds the degradation but does not remove it (4.22 vs.\ 2.50 miss probes at $C=4M$, $\alpha=0.50$), and since only two values of $\tau$ were tested, we make no claim about an optimal threshold.
\end{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Mechanism 3: Search Acceleration via Early Exit (\texttt{rh-noearly} vs \texttt{rh})}:

At moderate load ($\alpha = 0.50$), this cuts mean unsuccessful probe depth from $2.499$ to $1.751$ ($30.0\%$ reduction). Under heavy load ($\alpha = 0.80$), its impact is dramatic: miss probes drop from $12.933$ to \textbf{3.398}---a \textbf{73.7\% reduction} in lookup depth at $C=4M$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
Several experimental factors and methodological constraints could influence the validity of our findings:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{JIT Compilation and JVM Warmup}: Benchmarking in Java is susceptible to HotSpot JIT compilation tiers and dynamic deoptimization. In all research questions (RQ1--RQ3), each configuration is launched in an isolated JVM process to prevent class-loading contamination. To mitigate cold-start distortion while avoiding code mutation, small warmup passes (1,000 queries) precede timed lookups, and dead-code elimination (DCE) is countered via an accumulated 64-bit checksum (\texttt{output\_check}). Furthermore, probe counting is conducted in a completely separate \texttt{count} execution pass, ensuring probe depth measurements remain mathematically deterministic and immune to JIT jitter. JIT optimizations may also favor contiguous primitive-array access in Linear Probing over pointer-based node traversal in Separate Chaining; we did not inspect the compiled code.
    \item \textbf{Garbage Collection (GC) Interference and Memory Telemetry}: Workloads with extensive object allocation can trigger unpredictable stop-the-world GC pauses. In open-addressing tables (\texttt{lp-tomb}, \texttt{lp-backshift}, \texttt{rh}), implementations use flat primitive arrays; in contrast, Separate Chaining (\texttt{sc} in RQ1) allocates individual \texttt{Node} instances. Peak heap usage is tracked via Java's \texttt{MemoryPoolMXBean}, but \texttt{heap\_peak\_bytes} covers the entire benchmark process, including workload bookkeeping such as the active-key array and the \texttt{seenKeys} set, as well as table and query data; it is not a table-only memory measurement. Explicit GC and peak-counter reset occur before table construction, not immediately before lookup timing, so peak telemetry should be interpreted as process-level high-water usage rather than an isolated table footprint.
    \item \textbf{Execution Timeout Safety and Asymptotic Boundary}: Each benchmark trial is governed by a strict 60-second process timeout ($60{,}000$\,ms). In the corrected RQ2 campaign, the longest complete process took $46.044$\,seconds at $C=4M$ for the contaminated-table miss-time run; all 48 processes completed without timeout. The query-only miss latency for that run was $18.224$\,seconds and must not be mistaken for total process time. As $\text{EMPTY} \to 0$ asymptotically as $C \to \infty$, unsuccessful lookups approach exhaustive $\mathcal{O}(M)$ scans; workloads may exceed a timeout depending on table size and query volume.
    \item \textbf{Machine and Hardware Workload Isolation}: All official measurements were performed on a single dedicated machine (11th Gen Intel Core i5-11500H @ 2.90~GHz, 32~GB RAM, Windows 11 Pro) with background user processes terminated and network services suspended. Running all algorithms under identical hardware configurations ensures meaningful cross-algorithmic comparisons.
    \item \textbf{Hash Function, Seed Diversity, and Timing Variance}: All implementations utilize the standard 64-bit Fibonacci hashing function $h(k) = (k \cdot \phi) \ggg (64 - p)$ with $\phi = \texttt{0x9E3779B97F4A7C15}$ (unsigned value $11400714819323198485$). RQ3 uses multiple seeds ($42, 1337, 20261019$); RQ2 currently uses only seed $42$ and one repeat, so its results do not estimate between-seed or between-repeat variability and should be interpreted as a controlled single-seed case study, not a median across runs. Timed configurations use one 1,000-query warmup pass and one measured pass per JVM; this reduces but does not eliminate JIT tiering and process-level timing variation. Future RQ2 replications with additional seeds and repeats are needed to quantify that uncertainty. Matching \texttt{output\_check} values validate lookup results but do not validate timing stability.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
This empirical study systematically evaluates collision resolution strategies in open-addressing and chained hash tables under high-churn transactional workloads. By comparing Separate Chaining, Linear Probing with tombstones, Linear Probing with Knuth Algorithm R backshift deletion, and Robin Hood hashing, we provide concrete engineering recommendations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Separate Chaining vs. Linear Probing (RQ1)}: Algorithmic probe count does not translate directly to lower execution latency in Java. Separate Chaining keeps probe counts below 2 at every tested load and is far more robust to clustered hashes, but it uses roughly $1.6\times$--$2.8\times$ more measured heap per key and pointer-chasing nodes. Linear Probing is faster only for successful lookups at $\alpha = 0.50$ ($36.9$ vs.\ $54.0$\,ns/op); at $\alpha \ge 0.75$, for unsuccessful lookups and for larger tables ($M \ge 2^{20}$) Separate Chaining was equal or faster in our single-repeat measurements.
    \item \textbf{Tombstone Pollution and Rebuilding (RQ2)}: In the measured single-seed workload with continuous deletions, tombstone accumulation depletes empty slots to $0.04\%$ and raises miss depth to $2,848.99$ probes at $C=4M$. The one-million-query miss batch takes $18.22$ seconds on the contaminated table versus $89.39$ ms on the Clean Table. RQ3 shows that periodic rebuilding bounds but does not remove this degradation (4.22 vs.\ 2.50 miss probes at $\alpha=0.50$, $C=4M$), whereas Knuth's Algorithm R (\texttt{lp-backshift}) removes tombstones altogether.
    \item \textbf{Robin Hood Hashing and Mechanism Ablation (RQ3)}: In the measured RQ3 workloads, Robin Hood hashing with early exit reduces mean unsuccessful-lookup probes by $\approx 30.0\%$ at moderate load ($\alpha = 0.50$) and by a dramatic \textbf{73.8\%} under heavy load ($\alpha = 0.80$, dropping from 12.98 down to 3.40 probes) compared to \texttt{rh-noearly} and \texttt{lp-backshift}, while cutting miss probes by \textbf{83.7\%} relative to the checkpoint-averaged \texttt{lp-tomb} value at $\alpha=0.80$. Elapsed lookup and total workload times do not show a uniform advantage for Robin Hood, so the benefit established here lies in probe count and tail displacement (Max DIB), which matters most for workloads dominated by absent-key queries.
\end{itemize}
